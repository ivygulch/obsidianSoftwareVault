To the WeightWatchers Mobile Product Engineering team,

Your posting describes an iOS app whose job is to help a member do a small thing every day: log a meal, record a walk, look at a weight trend, and keep going. I have shipped two consumer health apps built on that same loop. MyFactor let hemophilia patients record medically significant events and share them with their care providers. TherexRx gave physical therapists a way to prescribe individualized exercise plans that rehabilitation patients follow at home. Both were independent engagements where I owned the iOS app end to end, including the data model, the backend synchronization, and the App Store releases. That is the closest work in my career to the behavior-change features you describe, and it is the reason I am writing.

The larger part of my record is consumer scale. As Principal Software Engineer on Walmart's checkout platform, I owned the architecture and technical standards for the checkout flow in the flagship iOS app, which serves more than five million people a day, in an organization of 120+ iOS engineers. Your "robust, modular, and testable codebase" line is the work I did there: unidirectional, reactive architecture in Swift and Combine with MVVM-C, Swift Package Manager modularization, and an automated testing discipline with XCTest and XCUITest that raised coverage to about 85 percent with a measurable drop in post-release defects. Every new checkout feature shipped behind a feature flag, most as A/B experiments measuring customer and business impact, and I owned the analytics and dashboards that showed whether a change helped. I would approach a health feature the same way: instrument it, flag it, and use member behavior to decide.

On modern Apple frameworks, my 2026 work is SwiftUI and Swift 6.2 concurrency. At CLEAR I planned and executed a phased migration of a production iOS app to Swift 6.2 concurrency alongside a six-engineer team, used SwiftUI for new feature work, and maintained the team's Fastlane CI/CD configuration. For a volunteer fire department, in a separate solo project, I built a local-first SwiftUI client that synchronizes across devices, covered it with Swift Testing, and translated a non-technical customer's requirements into formal specifications before writing code. Explaining trade-offs to partners who do not write software was a large part of getting that project shipped. I have been building on this platform since 2010 and have stayed current through each framework transition: Objective-C to Swift, RxSwift to Combine, UIKit to SwiftUI. In 2014 I gave a CocoaConf session on creative uses of the then-new iOS extensions, the ancestor of the widget and Live Activity work you list.

I have not shipped HealthKit integrations in production, but have experimented in local apps as I learn new frameworks. I have assisted teams with Lock Screen Widgets and Live Activities in production, but have not led those efforts. I would expect to be productive on WidgetKit, ActivityKit, and HealthKit quickly for your team.

Mentoring is familiar ground. I mentored 12+ engineers at Walmart, built the onboarding materials the iOS organization used, and served as interview bar-raiser for dozens of candidates a year. On AI, I sat on Walmart's working groups evaluating AI development tools and trained more than eighty engineers on the workflows that cleared the bar. I use Claude Code, Codex, and GitHub Spec Kit daily, and I can walk you through how I use them for research and development as well as what I accept and what I reject or modify from the tools.

Your posting mentions a hybrid work environment without naming an office. I am not within commuting distance of a WeightWatchers office, but am open to periodic visits. If hybrid means regular presence at a specific location, I would rather settle that early than after we have both invested in the process.

I would welcome a conversation with the hiring manager about the tracking and insights features on the roadmap and where the architecture needs to go to carry them.

Doug Sjoquist
