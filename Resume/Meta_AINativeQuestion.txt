I have not shipped a product where an AI model is the core user-facing functionality, and I want to be direct about that. My AI experience is on two adjacent fronts, one of which put AI-generated artifacts into a production path.

The closest to AI-native: at Walmart I helped the platform team evaluate and integrate Builder.io, which connected Walmart's design library and Figma to AI-generated iOS views. The generated code shipped to users in a flagship app, so the responsibility questions were real even though the AI ran at build time rather than runtime. We approached it by defining evaluation criteria before adoption, running hands-on usage assessments against real iOS workflows, and keeping a human acceptance gate — engineers reviewed and owned every generated view before it merged. Engineering management adopted it as the standard for new iOS view work only after that evaluation, and the same process rejected most of the Xcode-focused AI plugins we assessed as deficient for iOS-specific work.

The unique challenges we hit: generated output that looked plausible but violated the design system or accessibility expectations, which is why review criteria and training mattered more than the tool itself; and calibrating trust across 120+ engineers with different experience levels, which I addressed by training more than eighty of them on what to accept, what to reject, and what to modify.

The second front is agentic development, where AI-produced specifications and code are load-bearing in my daily work — GitHub Spec Kit and Claude Code took a volunteer fire department's incident-management app from a non-technical customer's requirements to a shipped 1.0. The discipline is the same one I would bring to an AI-native product: the model accelerates, a human owns the output, and quality review is designed in rather than assumed.
