To the Rythm mobile team,

Your posting says the mobile app's job is to make complex health information significantly easier for customers to understand and use, and that the person who owns it should set technical direction while staying close to the code. That is the shape of my last five years. As Principal Software Engineer on Walmart's checkout platform, I was responsible for the checkout flow in the flagship iOS app, which more than five million people use each day — the screen where a customer has to understand exactly what is about to happen and know, right away and correctly, whether it went through. I owned its technical direction: architecture evolution, modularization, and the long-term maintainability of a system other teams built on. I wrote and reviewed code in it the whole time.

On owning an app from architecture through launch and ongoing improvement, I have done it at three scales. At Walmart, every new checkout feature launched behind a feature flag, most as A/B experiments that measured customer and business impact, and I owned the analytics that judged them — instrumentation, dashboards, and monitoring through production rollout. For a volunteer fire department, in a separate solo project this year, I took an incident-management app from a non-technical customer's requirements to a shipped 1.0: a local-first SwiftUI client with Swift 6 concurrency that synchronizes across devices, against a Supabase backend I built and operated. And in consumer health specifically, I was the iOS developer for two apps as an independent: MyFactor for BioRx, where patients tracked medically significant events such as hemophilia bleeds and shared them with their care providers, which I maintained across multiple App Store releases over three years; and TherexRx for Clinically Relevant, an early-stage startup, where I architected the synchronization between the iOS client, the backend API, and a Realm-backed distributed data layer and took its auto-renewable subscriptions through App Store review.

On how a team builds — testing, releases, performance, security, and monitoring — the numbers I was accountable for at Walmart were a 99.9%+ crash-free session rate with roughly 90% of core checkout flows instrumented, held through traffic spikes, and test coverage raised to about 85% through an automated testing discipline that produced a measurable drop in post-release defects. I defined the iOS engineering standards for architecture, performance, security, and testing that a 120+ engineer organization built against across three apps, and led release readiness on a large inner-sourced codebase with frequent production releases. My most recent contract was at CLEAR, the identity-verification company, where I planned and executed a phased migration of the production iOS app to Swift 6 concurrency alongside a six-engineer team and maintained the Fastlane release automation.

On APIs, authentication, analytics, notifications, and third-party services: at Walmart I collaborated with backend teams on the GraphQL schema and mobile-optimized APIs for checkout and payments. At McGraw-Hill's Sharpen I delivered the authentication and onboarding features, integrated five analytics tools, built internal tooling for email, SMS, and push notifications, and helped tie RevenueCat subscriptions to the user's account rather than the device, syncing entitlement state to the backend after purchase and restore with retry handling.

On technical leadership through execution and communication: design review and pull-request review were daily work at Walmart. I mentored more than a dozen engineers, built the onboarding materials, served as an interview bar-raiser, and taught accessibility sessions across the iOS organization. On AI-assisted workflows, I trained more than 80 Walmart iOS engineers on them, used Claude Code, Codex, and GitHub Spec Kit across the fire-department and CLEAR work from planning through deployment, and can walk through how I used the tools for research and development as well as what I accepted and what I rejected or modified.

Two things to be direct about. First, my healthcare work is those two independent apps, both small and both some years ago; the sensitive-data environments I have worked in since are payments at Walmart and biometric identity at CLEAR. I have not worked with lab results or inside a lab, and I would be learning that domain from your team. Second, I am a native iOS engineer. At Walmart I explored the Android and web codebases regularly to keep iOS checkout aligned across platforms, but I have not shipped Android or cross-platform code, and iOS is where I would carry the architecture. If owning Rythm's mobile apps means one engineer owning both native platforms, I would want to settle that early.

The posting is a Staff role and my last title was Principal. Titles have never decided for me; leading through building on a small team is the work I want to do. The role is remote-first, and I am a remote candidate in the US.

I would welcome a conversation about what the app has to do once a lab result lands on a customer's phone, and where the mobile architecture needs to go to carry the next phase.
