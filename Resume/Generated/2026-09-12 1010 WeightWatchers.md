## Cover letter

To the WeightWatchers Mobile Product Engineering team,

Your posting describes an iOS app whose job is to help a member do a small thing every day: log a meal, record a walk, look at a weight trend, and keep going. I have shipped two consumer health apps built on exactly that loop. MyFactor let hemophilia patients record medically significant events and share them with their care providers, and TherexRx gave physical therapists a way to prescribe individualized exercise plans that rehabilitation patients follow at home. Both were independent engagements where I owned the iOS app end to end, including the data model, the backend synchronization, and App Store release. That is the closest thing in my career to the behavior-change work you describe, and it is the reason I am writing.

The larger half of my record is consumer scale. As Principal Software Engineer on Walmart's checkout platform, I owned the architecture and technical standards for the flow in the flagship iOS app that more than five million people use each day, in an organization of 120+ iOS engineers. Your "robust, modular, and testable codebase" line is the work I did there: unidirectional, reactive architecture in Swift and Combine with MVVM, Swift Package Manager modularization, and an automated testing discipline with XCTest and XCUITest that raised coverage to about 85 percent with a measurable drop in post-release defects. Every new checkout feature shipped behind a feature flag, most as A/B experiments measuring customer and business impact, and I owned the analytics and dashboards that told us whether a change actually helped. That is also how I would approach "questioning assumptions" on a health feature: instrument it, flag it, and let member behavior settle the argument.

On modern Apple frameworks, my 2026 work is all SwiftUI and Swift 6.2 concurrency. At CLEAR I planned and executed a phased migration of a production iOS app to Swift 6.2 concurrency alongside a six-engineer team, and maintained the team's Fastlane CI/CD configuration. For a volunteer fire department, in a separate solo project, I built a local-first SwiftUI client that synchronizes across devices, covered it with Swift Testing, and translated a non-technical customer's requirements into formal specifications before writing code. Explaining trade-offs to partners who do not write software is a large part of how that project got shipped. I have been building on this platform since 2010 and have kept current through every framework transition, from Objective-C to Swift, RxSwift to Combine, and UIKit to SwiftUI; I gave a CocoaConf session in 2014 on creative uses of the then-new iOS extensions, which is the ancestor of the widget and Live Activity work you list.

Where I want to be direct: I have not shipped HealthKit integrations, Lock Screen Widgets, or Live Activities in production. What I bring to those initiatives is the data-integration and synchronization experience from the health apps above, the habit of reading each WWDC's changes closely, and a record of adopting new Apple frameworks in production ahead of the crowd. I would expect to be productive on WidgetKit and ActivityKit quickly, and I would rather say that plainly than imply otherwise.

Mentoring is familiar ground: I mentored 12+ engineers at Walmart, built the onboarding materials the iOS organization used, and served as interview bar-raiser for dozens of candidates a year. On AI, I sat on Walmart's working groups evaluating AI development tools and trained more than eighty engineers on the workflows that cleared the bar, and I use Claude Code, Codex, and GitHub Spec Kit daily with the verification habits that come from testing at scale.

Your posting mentions a hybrid work environment without naming an office. I am not within commuting distance of a WeightWatchers office, but I am open to periodic visits. If hybrid means regular presence at a specific location, I would like to settle that early rather than after we have both invested in the process.

I would welcome a conversation with the hiring manager about the tracking and insights features on the roadmap and where the architecture needs to go to carry them.

Doug Sjoquist
