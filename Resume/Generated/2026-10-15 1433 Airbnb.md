To the iOS Platform team at Airbnb,

Your posting describes a role that improves AI agents' effectiveness across the development workflow for a large, nearly all-Swift iOS codebase. The last fifteen months of my Walmart tenure were that work from the product-engineering side. As Principal Software Engineer on the checkout platform, I began experimenting with AI development tooling in late 2024 on my own initiative, before it was on any roadmap: Microsoft Copilot and Visual Studio first, then a series of Xcode plugins that mostly turned out to be deficient for iOS-specific workflows, then design-integrated tooling as the options matured. That grew into the engineering working groups that evaluated AI tools for the organization, where I assisted the platform team in evaluating and integrating Builder.io, connecting Walmart's design library with Figma to generate and update iOS views, and led training sessions on it for over 80 iOS engineers. Engineering management adopted it as the standard for new view work across an organization of 120+ iOS engineers.

I heavily used Claude Code and OpenAI Codex at CLEAR, the identity-verification company, to analyze their iOS codebase for architectural patterns, technical debt, and improvement opportunities, then planned and executed a phased migration of the production app to Swift 6 concurrency alongside a six-engineer team. For a volunteer fire department, in a separate solo project, I used GitHub Spec Kit to drive spec-first development and Claude Code, Codex, and XcodeBuildMCP for implementation, taking a local-first SwiftUI client with Swift 6 concurrency and a Supabase backend from a non-technical customer's requirements to a shipped 1.0, covered with Swift Testing.

At Walmart I owned the architecture for checkout in the flagship app, which more than five million people use each day. The codebase was structured into platform and feature modules that were shared across apps for multiple markets. The app architecture was built around a unidirectional, reactive design in Swift and Combine. I helped define the iOS engineering standards for architecture, performance, security, and testing that the 120+ engineer organization built against across three apps, raised checkout test coverage to about 85%, and held a 99.9%+ crash-free session rate with roughly 90% of core flows instrumented. Earlier, I migrated the Sam's Club Objective-C codebase to Swift with uninterrupted service for roughly two million daily users.

I want to be clear about my experience with Go and Ruby. I have only played with sample apps in Go, but I am certain I would quickly become proficient enough to get things done. I have used Ruby, but it has been several years since I did any serious work with it. My tooling and backend work in the past has primarily been in the Java and database worlds. Regarding agent-workflow efficiency as a measured discipline, I have not instrumented or optimized agent execution time or token efficiency as a product; my work on agent effectiveness has been at the practice level, deciding which tools fit iOS workflows and which do not, structuring spec-first work so agents produce reviewable results, and verifying their output against an engineering bar. Walmart was moving toward Bazel during my last few months, but I have not used Bazel in production.

I am a remote candidate in the US and can travel to San Francisco for a week each quarter.

I would welcome a conversation about where agent effectiveness is breaking down in your iOS workflow today and which of those problems the platform team wants solved first.

Doug Sjoquist
